# Theory of Constraints for Public-Sector Work (ReEngine Consulting)

## 0. When to use this skill

Use when:
- Work is piling up somewhere and leaders keep adding effort everywhere (overtime, new reports, more meetings) without the backlog falling.
- A scarce expert is saturated: judge, engineer, hydrologist, attorney, adjudicator, counselor, inspector, licensing specialist.
- Staff run many projects at once (bad multitasking) and nothing finishes.
- A chronic conflict persists ("centralize or decentralize," "prevent fraud or make it accessible," "track tasks or trust professionals").
- Leadership needs a one-page logical plan (Strategic Path) that experts agree is sufficient to reach the goal.
- A budget request needs to show whether money is really the constraint.
- Capacity modeling, backlog clearance projections, or staffing justifications are required.
- A project portfolio needs reliable delivery (CCPM).

Do not use alone when:
- Flow is basically fine and waste is the issue (lean.md).
- The problem is decision inconsistency or variation without a clear bottleneck (six-sigma.md).
- The constraint is known and the solution designed, but people are not adopting it (change-management.md).

ReEngine stance: TOC is the "Focus" binding element of the 4 Pillars framework (Purpose, Focus, Value). GQI blends TOC with Lean and Six Sigma; TOC tells you where to aim.

## 2. Core concepts

### 2.1 The premise

Every system that has a goal has at least one constraint: the factor that limits its performance relative to the goal. If there were no constraint, throughput would be infinite. Because systems are chains of dependent events subject to variation, a few points govern overall performance. Improving anything other than the constraint produces local efficiencies, not system results.

### 2.2 The pillars of TOC (Baptista, BOK)

The BOK chapter on the pillars sets out the beliefs that underpin TOC. The two most used at ReEngine:
- Inherent Simplicity: the more complex a system looks, the simpler it is, because the interdependencies mean a few elements (the constraint and the core problem) govern the whole. Day and Zimmermann summarized it as "Every Situation is Simple": complex systems are governed by very few points of leverage [Summary].
- Inherent Consistency (no real conflicts): conflicts are based on erroneous assumptions that can be surfaced and challenged; win-win is possible. This is why the evaporating cloud works.
Other pillars commonly listed: people are good (blame is the wrong lens; conflicts stem from the system), every situation can be substantially improved (the sky is not the limit), and never say "I know" (keep learning; a solution today can be challenged tomorrow).

### 2.3 Types of constraints

| Type | Description | Public-sector examples |
|---|---|---|
| Physical / resource | A person, role, equipment, or space | Judge time; engineering review; hydrology review; courtroom; inspector |
| Market (demand) | Not enough demand | Rare in government, since demand is usually uncontrollable and exceeds capacity; can apply to voluntary programs |
| Policy | Rules, measures, behaviors, habits | Approval required for every waiver; "first in, first out regardless of complexity"; denial-only status for incomplete files; incentive to look busy |
| Paradigm | Mindsets and assumptions | "Everyone must be fully utilized"; "more review equals more quality" |
| Information | Missing, late, or wrong information | OWBC: the constraint was information accuracy, not staff capacity [Summary] |

In government, policy constraints are the most common and most powerful. Goldratt's observation that most constraints are policy constraints is confirmed by ReEngine engagements: NYSDOT's lesson "subordinate the waiver to the audit" moved from approving every request in real time to retroactive audits [Summary].

### 2.4 Unique features of government (Mycue and Schragenheim, BOK)

The BOK paper identifies characteristics that make government different and shape how TOC applies:

| Feature | What it means | TOC implication |
|---|---|---|
| An elusive goal | Government goals are multiple, contested, and hard to measure | Use the Strategic Compass and a goal tree to define the goal unit and necessary conditions; define quality throughput |
| Unchallengeable regulations | Statutes and rules cannot be changed quickly by managers | Treat as necessary conditions or as external constraints; challenge internal policy interpretations instead |
| Stable funding | Budgets are set annually or biennially; throughput does not generate revenue | Throughput accounting must be reframed (QT/OE); exploit before elevate |
| Government cannot control demand | Applications, cases, arrests, disasters arrive regardless of capacity | Choke the release of work into the system where legally possible (Full-Kit gating, scheduling) and manage queues visibly |
| Moore's Strategic Triangle | Public value, legitimacy and support, operational capacity must align | Solutions must satisfy all three; include the authorizing environment |
| Money must be paired with authority | Funding without decision authority does not deliver | Map decision rights; NYSDOT's ODO held accountability without authority over upstream goal-setting [Summary] |
| A duty in disasters | Government must respond in surges and emergencies | Design surge capacity and buffers; WY DEQ targeted an adaptive resilient system for a 30% throughput increase [Target] |

The paper also presents a generic conflict cloud for government: the need to protect against corruption and misuse (leading to controls, approvals, and documentation) versus the need for quick response to citizens (leading to delegation and speed). Many government processes embody one side of this cloud. The resolution often lies in post-decision audit, risk-based controls, and delegated authority with standards, which protect against misuse without front-loading approvals on every transaction.

The paper further discusses whether the budget is the constraint. Usually it is not, until the existing resources at the constraint are fully exploited. Before asking for more money (elevate), show that the constraint is not wasting time on non-constraint work, rework, and interruptions.

It also illustrates weighting of quality throughput: for example, Class A and Class B investigations may carry different weights in QT so that the measure reflects public value rather than raw counts.

### 2.5 The five focusing steps (POOGI)

0. Define the goal and the measure (government: the goal unit and quality throughput).
1. Identify the constraint.
2. Decide how to exploit the constraint (get the most from it without big spending).
3. Subordinate everything else to that decision (non-constraints pace to the constraint and protect it).
4. Elevate the constraint (add capacity: staff, technology, contracts, schedules) if still needed.
5. If the constraint is broken, go back to step 1; do not let inertia become the constraint.

Detailed guidance:

Step 1, identify:
- Where does WIP accumulate? (Journey Board, cumulative flow, aging reports.)
- Which role do others wait on? Who has the longest queue relative to capacity?
- Load vs capacity per resource (hours of demand per week vs available hours).
- Ask staff: "If you had one more person, where would you put them?"
- Watch for wandering constraints caused by batching or multitasking.
- Distinguish the constraint from the symptom: the longest queue may sit in front of a step starved by policy elsewhere.
- Constraint within the constraint: WY DEQ Coal identified technical review as the general bottleneck and hydrology and CHIA reviews as the "constraint-squared" [Summary].

Step 2, exploit:
- Never let the constraint wait for work: maintain a buffer of ready (Full-Kit) work in front of it.
- Never let the constraint work on bad inputs: quality check before the constraint (Front Door, Full-Kit).
- Remove non-constraint tasks from the constraint (delegate, coordinator roles, automation). Albany: delegation of sentencing authority for plea negotiations to the City Solicitor and a Cite and Release standard removed hundreds of Red Steps from the judge's queue [Summary]. Columbus Traffic: a Study Coordinator role protected engineers' capacity for technical review [Summary].
- Protect constraint time: schedules, focus blocks, fewer interruptions (Greeley confirmation briefs and back-briefs [Summary]).
- Do not waste constraint time on rework: deficiency loops that return to the constraint cost twice.
- Prioritize constraint work by value per constraint minute (or due date for legal clocks).
- Run the constraint during breaks or overtime first, before any other step.

Step 3, subordinate:
- Release work into the system only at the pace the constraint can handle (choke and release; drum-buffer-rope). SD BIT implemented choke-and-release logic so the system was fed only what the development constraint could handle [Summary].
- Non-constraints may have idle time; that is correct and expected. Do not measure them on utilization.
- Upstream steps prepare Full-Kit work; downstream steps clear the constraint's output promptly.
- Policies and measures must support the constraint (for example, stop measuring intake on volume processed if it pushes incomplete files forward).
- "Subordinate to the X" is a ReEngine principle: subordinate to the bench (Albany), the counselor (NH VR), the engineer (Santa Clara Valley Water, Columbus Traffic), the decision (Greeley), the chain of custody (Pittsburgh Police), the student (SD BOR), the document (OWBC), the goal (Columbus 3P) [Summary].

Step 4, elevate:
- Add capacity: staff, contractors, overtime, technology, a full-time judge, extended hours.
- Albany's WGLL game resulted in a recommendation for a full-time (40-hour) judge and a 5-day hybrid schedule [Target; recommendation].
- Elevate only after exploit and subordinate, and show the math: the BOK government paper's point that budget usually is not the constraint until resources are exploited.

Step 5, repeat and avoid inertia:
- When the constraint moves, policies built for the old constraint can become the new constraint.
- Re-run identification at each 30/60/90-day review.

### 2.6 Throughput accounting and QT/OE

Classic TOC measures:
- Throughput (T): rate at which the system generates money through sales (selling price minus truly variable costs).
- Investment or inventory (I): money tied up in things intended to be sold.
- Operating expense (OE): money spent turning I into T.
- Net profit = T - OE; ROI = (T - OE) / I; productivity = T / OE.
Decision rule: an action is good if it increases T, or decreases I or OE, without hurting the others; T is prioritized because it is unbounded.

Government adaptation (ReEngine, BOK paper): government does not sell; it produces public value. Replace T with quality throughput (QT): the count of goal units completed meeting quality criteria, optionally weighted by value class. Productivity becomes QT/OE.

Worked example from the BOK paper: an operation that completes 100 units at 95% quality with operating expense of $9,800 has QT/OE = (0.95 x 100) / 9,800 = 0.0097 quality units per dollar, which is about $103 per quality unit. If QT rises 25% while OE rises 10%, QT/OE improves by 1.25 / 1.10 - 1 = about 13.6% (the paper reports about 13.5%). "Value realized" illustration: 25 additional quality units x $103 = $2,575 per month. Remember the civil-rights investigation example: cost is not value; an investigation's cost (about $4,500 in the illustration) does not measure the protection it delivers.

How to use QT/OE in engagements:
- Define the goal unit (permit issued correctly, case resolved, claim paid accurately, inspection completed to standard).
- Define the quality criteria (correct, complete, timely, legally defensible).
- Measure QT per period, and OE for the value stream (staff cost, contractors, systems).
- Compare scenarios: VEC built a financial visualization model correlating operating expenses (FTEs, contractors, dollars) with quality throughput units [Summary]; WY DEQ LQD's ePermit conversion plan compared three resource scenarios (internal overtime, temporary staff with QC, fixed-term staff) in days vs dollars [Summary].

### 2.7 Variation, dependency, and why balanced capacity fails

In a chain of dependent steps with variation, delays accumulate and gains do not (a late upstream step makes downstream late; an early one rarely makes downstream early because work waits). A balanced line (all steps equal capacity) is therefore unstable; an unbalanced line with a deliberate constraint and protective capacity at non-constraints flows better. The Bead Game (or dice game) simulation demonstrates this, as ReEngine did with Day and Zimmermann [Summary].

Over-scheduling to protect a constraint: Albany's recommendation was that a docket should not be perfectly scheduled; over-scheduling (to 133%) protects against statistical fluctuations in defendant attendance [Target; recommendation only]. The logic: the constraint (bench time) must not starve when some defendants fail to appear.

## 3. Drum-Buffer-Rope and Simplified DBR

### 3.1 Classic DBR

- Drum: the constraint's schedule sets the pace of the whole system.
- Buffer: time (and the work it represents) placed in front of the constraint, and before shipping or due dates, to protect against variation.
- Rope: the release mechanism that ties the release of new work into the system to the drum, so WIP does not flood the floor.

### 3.2 Simplified DBR (S-DBR)

S-DBR (Schragenheim and Dettmer) treats demand (due dates) as the main constraint for planning and uses a single shipping buffer, releasing work a buffer-length before the due date, with the capacity-constrained resource monitored via a planned load check. For government:
- The "market" is the statutory or service-level due date (for example a 30-day review clock, a speedy-trial window, a 45-day eligibility determination).
- Release work (start the clock where legally allowed) only when Full-Kit; release according to the due date minus buffer time.
- Monitor planned load at the scarce resource (how many hours of judge, engineer, or adjudicator time are committed for the next N weeks) before committing new dates.

### 3.3 Applying DBR to dockets and review queues

| DBR element | Court docket | Permit review | Benefits adjudication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drum | Judge bench time per session | Senior engineer review hours | Adjudicator decisions per week |
| Constraint buffer | Ready bench: cases with full file, parties confirmed, staged in a "chute" (Albany) [Summary] | Queue of Full-Kit applications ready for review | Ready queue of claims with fact-finding complete |
| Shipping buffer | Time between hearing and statutory deadline | Time between target and legal deadline | Time to statutory payment deadline |
| Rope | Scheduling rules; calendar caps; standing orders | Intake release only when Full-Kit; pre-application meeting (MOU) | Release to adjudication only after fact-finding |
| Protective capacity | Clerks, solicitor, bailiffs | Coordinators, technicians | Intake staff, call center |

Albany municipal court illustrated several of these: an 8:40 AM standup, a chute and ready bench, batch standing orders, a booking data standard, and Full-Kit cases before the gavel drops at 8:40 so the judge's time is maximized [Summary; booking standard verified per recommendations report].

### 3.4 Choke and release

Limit the number of items in process to what the constraint can handle, plus the buffer. SD BIT's development division (153 FTEs) faced demand far exceeding capacity and bad multitasking; ReEngine identified development as the constraint and implemented choke-and-release logic with a takt board [Summary]. The lesson recorded: there is no value in pushing more work into Analysis if Development is already at capacity; it only increases WIP and slows the system [Summary].

Where the law requires accepting all applications, choke at the point of release into active review, not at receipt: accept, acknowledge, check Full-Kit, and queue visibly. Minnesota childcare licensing proposed an "Official Starting Line" so the licensing clock starts only when a Full-Kit application is submitted [Summary].

## 4. Buffer management

### 4.1 Concept

A buffer is a time allowance. Buffer consumption tells you priority and process health:
- Green (first third of buffer consumed): normal; no action.
- Yellow (second third): prepare; check what is needed.
- Red (last third): expedite; act now.
- Black (beyond buffer): late; escalate and learn.

### 4.2 Using buffer status

- Priority rule: work in red goes first, regardless of arrival order. This replaces FIFO-by-default and "squeaky wheel" expediting.
- Daily huddle: review all red items; assign actions.
- Process improvement: record reasons for red penetration (Pareto of causes). Fix the top causes with Lean and Six Sigma. This is the TOC link to continuous improvement.
- Buffer sizing: start with a reasonable portion of current lead time (often half of the current quoted time in production, adjusted for legal clocks in government); adjust if too many items are red (buffer too small or capacity issue) or almost none (buffer too large).

### 4.3 Buffer management in government (hypothetical example)

A permitting office has a 30-day legal review clock. It sets a 15-day internal buffer: target issuance by day 15, with green days 1-5, yellow 6-10, red 11-15, black beyond 15 but still within the legal clock. Huddles review red items daily. After six weeks, the Pareto of red causes shows missing utility sign-off as the top reason; the team creates a Full-Kit item and a standard request letter for utility sign-off. (Hypothetical example; no real figures.)

Cross-reference ReEngine practice: Journey Boards with weekly targets and WGLL O-G-P times function as visual buffer management (lean.md Section 11), and the BRAG tool is a buffer-like status for improvement actions.

## 5. Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM)

### 5.1 Why projects are late

- Padding: each task estimate includes safety, but safety is wasted by Student Syndrome (starting late) and Parkinson's Law (work expands to fill time).
- Bad multitasking: switching among projects delays all of them.
- Dependencies and resource contention: a late task delays successors; early finishes are not passed on.

### 5.2 CCPM method

1. Build the project network (tasks and dependencies).
2. Estimate task durations aggressively but feasibly (often about half the padded estimate; or the 50% confidence estimate).
3. Resolve resource conflicts: the critical chain is the longest chain of dependent tasks considering both task and resource dependencies.
4. Aggregate safety into a project buffer at the end of the critical chain (commonly about half of the removed safety, or the square root of sum of squares method).
5. Add feeding buffers where non-critical chains join the critical chain.
6. Add resource buffers (alerts) so critical resources are ready.
7. Execute with relay-race behavior: start when inputs arrive, work full speed, hand off as soon as done; no milestones per task.
8. Manage with a fever chart: percent of critical chain complete vs percent of project buffer consumed. Green, yellow, red zones drive action.
9. Multi-project: stagger project starts according to the strategic resource (pipelining; the drum for the portfolio). Freeze a share of projects to reduce WIP if needed.

### 5.3 CCPM in government

- Legislative deadlines, grant periods, and procurement rules create fixed dates; buffers make promises reliable.
- IT portfolios suffer bad multitasking (SD BIT developers spread across too many projects [Summary]).
- Engagement planning: ReEngine multi-program engagements (WY DEQ six programs with mini-launch sessions [Summary]) benefit from staggered starts so the client's subject-matter experts (often the constraint) are not overwhelmed.
- See project-management.md for integration with PMBOK scheduling, EVM, and contract reporting.

Fever chart reading (hypothetical example): 40% of critical chain complete with 60% of project buffer consumed sits in red; action: recovery plan, add resource, or reduce scope with sponsor approval.

## 6. The Thinking Processes

The Thinking Processes (TP) answer three questions: What to change? To what to change? How to cause the change? Many practitioners extend them with Zultner's and others' framing of six questions on the layers of resistance (the BOK includes Zultner, RENOvated Six Questions, 2025, identified but not read in full).

### 6.1 Categories of Legitimate Reservation (CLR)

The logic rules used to scrutinize every TP diagram:
1. Clarity: is the statement understood?
2. Entity existence: does it exist?
3. Causality existence: does the cause really lead to the effect?
4. Cause insufficiency: is an additional cause needed (the "and" connector)?
5. Additional cause: could another cause independently produce the effect?
6. Cause-effect reversal: is it backward?
7. Predicted effect existence: if the cause exists, what else should we see? Do we?
8. Tautology: is the effect used as proof of the cause?
Use CLR in workshops as respectful challenges: "Help me understand: does X always lead to Y, or does something else need to be true too?"

### 6.2 Current Reality Tree (CRT)

Purpose: find the core problem(s) behind many undesirable effects (UDEs).
Steps:
1. List 5-10 UDEs (present tense, factual, about the system: "Applicants wait months for decisions," "Reviewers re-review resubmittals," "Staff overtime is chronic").
2. Connect UDEs with if-then logic upward and downward, adding entities as needed.
3. Scrutinize with CLR.
4. Find the entity or entities that lead to most UDEs (the core problem); often a policy or a conflict.
5. Validate with the people who live it.
ReEngine's interference diagram (lean.md Section 15.4) is a faster, workshop-friendly entry point: list interferences to the goal, then cluster and connect; use the CRT when the logic must be rigorous.

### 6.3 Evaporating Cloud (EC, conflict resolution diagram)

Structure:
- A: common objective.
- B and C: needs (requirements) each side must protect to reach A.
- D and D': conflicting wants (prerequisites) that seem necessary to satisfy B and C.
- Arrows: B requires D; C requires D'; D conflicts with D'.

Steps:
1. State the conflict D vs D' in concrete terms.
2. Ask why each side wants it (B, C).
3. Find the common objective A.
4. Read the cloud aloud ("In order to A, we must B. In order to B, we must D. ...").
5. Surface assumptions under each arrow ("We must D because ...").
6. Challenge the weakest assumption with an injection (an idea that makes an assumption invalid).
7. Check that the injection satisfies both B and C; check negative branches.

ReEngine examples from the RAG:
- MaineHousing HEAP: perceived conflict between preventing fraud and making the program accessible; the cloud showed self-attestation with back-end audits could satisfy both needs [Summary]. (Confirm with David before external use.)
- Minnesota childcare licensing: the state needed centralization for consistency while counties needed decentralization for local flexibility; the cloud supported a state-hosted, county-based quality assurance network concept [Summary].
- Greeley City Attorney's Office: "track or don't track to-dos"; the cloud surfaced management's need for situational awareness and staff's need for trust and professional space; resolution routed routine tasks via paralegals using standardized criteria [Summary].
- The generic government cloud (BOK paper): protect against corruption vs respond quickly; injections such as post-decision audits (NYSDOT's 7-day objection-based waiver window and retroactive audits [Target]).

Template:

```
A (common objective): ____________________________
B (need 1): ______________   C (need 2): ______________
D (want 1): ______________   D' (want 2): _____________
Conflict: D vs D'
Assumptions:
  A-B: ____   A-C: ____   B-D: ____   C-D': ____   D-D' (why they cannot coexist): ____
Injection(s): ____________________________
Check: does the injection protect B? C? Negative branches?
```

### 6.4 Future Reality Tree (FRT) and Negative Branch Reservation (NBR)

FRT: start with injections; show logically that they produce desirable effects (DEs, the opposites of UDEs); add injections until sufficient. NBR: for each injection, ask "what could go wrong?" and trim the negative branch with an additional injection. Example: injection "self-attestation for high-friction documents"; negative branch "fraud increases"; trimming injection "risk-based back-end audit sample with clear consequences."

### 6.5 Prerequisite Tree (PRT) and Transition Tree (TT)

PRT: list obstacles to implementing the injection; for each obstacle, define an intermediate objective (IO) that overcomes it; sequence the IOs. TT: detailed step-by-step actions with the logic of why each action produces the needed change, used for complex or sensitive rollouts.

### 6.6 Goal Tree (Intermediate Objectives Map) and ReEngine Strategic Path

Goal Tree (Dettmer): Goal at top; critical success factors below; necessary conditions below those; read with necessity logic ("In order to have X, we must have Y"). Compare current state to each element (green, yellow, red) to find gaps.

ReEngine Strategic Path (CORE_METH_Strategic-Path-Logic): moves from the interference diagram to a one-page logical roadmap using sufficiency logic. For each element from top to bottom, ask "Is completing this step sufficient to achieve the next step?" If not, "What else do we need?" The finished map is a plan the experts agree cannot help but reach the goal. Strategic Path Maps appear in many ReEngine deliverables: Prairie View (certified purchasing SMEs at department level as a necessary condition for Net30 compliance [Summary]), Columbus Traffic, NYSDOT (from transactional triage to programmatic assurance [Summary]).

Facilitation for a Strategic Path:
1. Post the goal (from the Strategic Compass).
2. Post the major interferences (from the interference diagram).
3. For each interference, write the intermediate objective that overcomes it.
4. Arrange IOs in sequence; read upward with sufficiency logic.
5. Add missing IOs where the group says "not sufficient."
6. Assign owners and dates; turn into the implementation plan (lean.md Section 15.5) and BRAG tracking.

### 6.7 Layers of resistance (buy-in sequence)

Common formulation:
1. Disagreement on the problem.
2. Disagreement on the direction of the solution.
3. Disagreement that the solution solves the problem.
4. "Yes, but" (the solution has negative consequences).
5. "Yes, but" (obstacles to implementation).
6. Unverbalized fears.
Address in order; do not present solutions before agreeing on the problem. This aligns with ADKAR Awareness and Desire (change-management.md). TOC tools map to layers: CRT and cloud for layers 1-2, FRT for 3, NBR for 4, PRT for 5, and open dialogue plus sponsor commitments for 6.

### 6.8 The six questions for technology (Goldratt) applied to system replacements

1. What is the power of the technology?
2. What limitation does it diminish?
3. What rules helped us accommodate the limitation?
4. What new rules should we use now?
5. In light of the change in rules, what changes are required in the technology?
6. How to cause the change?
Use with "clean before automating": many government IT projects digitize old rules built for paper limitations. SD Attorney General's Sex Offender Registry replacement required pre-automation process cleanup and clear IT business requirements [Summary].

## 7. TOC for backlogs and dockets: a procedure

1. Define the goal unit and quality (QT).
2. Map the flow end to end with volumes and WIP at each stage (lean.md VSM).
3. Measure load vs capacity at each stage (hours demanded vs available), including rework loops.
4. Identify the constraint (and any constraint within it).
5. Quantify: at current constraint throughput, when will the backlog clear? (Backlog divided by net clearance rate = throughput minus arrivals. If arrivals exceed throughput, it never clears.)
6. Exploit: Full-Kit, remove non-constraint work, protect time, eliminate rework into the constraint, prioritize by buffer status.
7. Subordinate: choke release, align non-constraint schedules, change measures.
8. Aged-case strategy: interrogate the outliers (NH VR "Old Dogs" [Summary]); close, transfer, or resolve stale cases with defined criteria; treat them as a separate project so they do not starve current flow.
9. Model scenarios: what if constraint capacity rises 10%? (VEC modeled a hypothetical 10% improvement in adjudication capacity to show a measurable reduction in backlog [Summary].)
10. Elevate if still needed, with QT/OE justification.
11. Install visual management (Journey Board, takt board, buffer colors) and huddles.
12. Re-identify the constraint at each review.

Worked backlog math (hypothetical example): backlog 1,200 cases; arrivals 300 per week; constraint throughput 320 per week. Net clearance 20 per week; clearance time 60 weeks. If exploitation raises throughput 10% to 352, net clearance becomes 52 per week and clearance time falls to about 23 weeks. A modest constraint improvement has a large effect on backlog clearance when arrivals are close to capacity. Conversely, a 10% improvement at a non-constraint changes nothing.

Little's Law check: average lead time equals WIP divided by throughput (1,200 / 320 = 3.75 weeks of queue at the constraint, plus other steps).

### 8.9 WGLL game (adapted from the Albany engagement)

Purpose: let decision-makers allocate the constraint's time to an ideal week.
1. Inventory every task the constraint performs weekly. (Albany: 177 distinct judicial tasks required weekly to maintain flow [Summary].)
2. Estimate time per task and frequency.
3. Give participants a week of time slots (tokens or blocks).
4. Have them "spend" the slots on an ideal week, removing or delegating tasks that do not need the constraint.
5. Compare with demand. The gap shows whether delegation, scheduling, or elevation is needed.
Albany's outcome was a new 5-day hybrid schedule concept and a recommendation for a full-time (40-hour) judge [Target; recommendation].

## 9. Metrics

- Quality throughput (QT) per period and QT/OE.
- Constraint utilization on value-adding work (percent of available constraint hours spent on constraint-only work).
- Constraint idle time due to starvation (no Full-Kit work available): goal zero.
- WIP and WIP age at and before the constraint.
- Lead time (median, 90th percentile).
- Due-date performance (percent within statutory or target clock).
- Buffer penetration distribution (percent green, yellow, red, black) and top red reasons.
- Rework rate returning to the constraint.
- Backlog clearance projection (weeks to clear at current net rate).
- For projects: fever chart status; project buffer consumption vs critical chain completion; number of active projects per person (multitasking).

Avoid local efficiency metrics at non-constraints (items processed per person at intake), which encourage pushing incomplete work forward.

## 12. Decision guides

### 12.1 Is there a constraint, and what kind?

| Evidence | Likely constraint type | First move |
|---|---|---|
| WIP piles in front of one role; others wait | Resource | Exploit: Full-Kit buffer, remove non-constraint work |
| Queues everywhere; nobody idle; many projects open | Bad multitasking / policy | Choke release; freeze some work |
| Work waits for approvals or sign-offs | Policy | Cloud the control vs speed conflict; delegate with standards; post-decision audit |
| Files return repeatedly for missing items | Information / policy | Full-Kit, Front Door, Pending status |
| Demand spikes cause collapse | Capacity with no surge design | Buffers, surge plan, cross-training |
| Leaders disagree on direction | Paradigm / conflict | Evaporating cloud; goal tree |

### 12.2 Exploit or elevate?

Exploit first unless: the constraint is already fully protected (verified), legal deadlines are being missed with harm, or a known capacity loss (vacancy) explains the gap. Then build an elevation case with QT/OE.

### 12.3 Which thinking-process tool?

- Chronic conflict between two positions: evaporating cloud.
- Many symptoms, unclear core problem: interference diagram, then CRT.
- Need a plan the experts trust: Strategic Path (sufficiency) or goal tree (necessity).
- Worried about side effects: NBR.
- Implementation obstacles: PRT.
- Very sensitive, detailed rollout: TT.

### 12.4 When is TOC the wrong lens?

When work is not flowing through dependent steps (independent casework with no bottleneck), when the issue is accuracy rather than throughput, or when constraint identification would mostly blame an individual. In the last case, reframe: the role is the constraint, not the person; protect the role.

## 13. Facilitation scripts and questions

Introducing the constraint idea:
"A chain is only as strong as its weakest link. In our process, one point determines how many cases we can finish each week. If we improve anything else, the total stays the same. Let's find that point together."

Protecting the constraint person (judge, attorney, engineer):
"We are not saying you're the problem. We're saying your time is the most valuable resource in this system, so everything else should be organized to protect it. What do you do today that someone else could do?"

Non-constraint staff:
"It's okay, and expected, that your step will sometimes have slack. Your new job is to make sure every file that reaches [constraint] is complete and ready. That's how we all finish more."

Cloud facilitation:
"Both of these positions make sense. Let's write down what each side is protecting. Read it with me: In order to [A], we must [B]; in order to [B], we must [D]. Why must we? What are we assuming?"

Challenging a policy constraint:
"Where does this rule come from: statute, rule, policy, or habit? If it's a policy, who owns it? What risk was it designed to address, and is there another way to manage that risk?"

Sufficiency check (Strategic Path):
"If we accomplish this step, is that enough to get to the next one? What else would have to be true?"

Questions bank:
- Where does work wait the longest?
- Who do people wait for?
- If you had one more person, where would they go?
- What fraction of the constraint's time goes to work only they can do?
- How often does work come back to the constraint?
- What rule makes us release work before it is complete?
- What would happen if we released less work?
- When the constraint is absent, what happens to the flow?

## 14. Common pitfalls

1. Improving non-constraints and expecting system results.
2. Identifying the constraint by opinion rather than load, WIP, and gemba.
3. Treating the constraint as a person to blame.
4. Elevating (asking for budget) before exploiting and subordinating.
5. Measuring non-constraints on utilization, causing WIP floods.
6. Forgetting the legal clocks and due-process requirements when choking release; choke at review release, not by refusing applications.
7. Ignoring the constraint moving after success (inertia).
8. Running clouds as debates; the point is assumptions, not winners.
9. Building logic trees no one in the agency can read; keep them short and validate with the people.
10. CCPM without behavior change (relay race, no multitasking) becomes just padded buffers.
11. Over-scheduling without managing the effect on people (attendees waiting); pair with staging and communication.
12. Presenting modeled projections as results.

## Appendix A. Domain playbooks (where the constraint usually hides)

These are starting hypotheses, not conclusions. Always verify with load, WIP, and gemba.

### A.1 Courts and dockets
- Usual constraint: judge or hearing-officer bench time; sometimes courtroom availability or interpreter availability.
- Common policy constraints: every matter requires a hearing; continuances granted freely; cases scheduled without confirming readiness; warrants and booking data incomplete.
- Exploit: ready bench and chute (cases staged with complete files); batch standing orders; delegate non-judicial decisions (solicitor plea authority, clerk processing); pre-session standup to confirm readiness; virtual hearings for appropriate matters.
- Subordinate: booking data standards upstream; clerk scheduling rules tied to bench capacity; over-scheduling calibrated to failure-to-appear rates.
- Measures: cases resolved per session, clearance rate (dispositions divided by filings), time to disposition, age of pending caseload, failure-to-appear rate, continuance rate.
- RAG reference: Albany (Section 10).

### A.2 Corrections
- Usual constraints: intake and classification staff; medical clearance; records verification; program seats; transport.
- Policy constraints: intake clock starts before records and medical are available; duplicate data entry across systems.
- Exploit and subordinate: Full-Kit for records and medical before the admission and orientation clock starts (SD DOC A&O [Summary]); scheduling transports to classification capacity.
- Measures: days in intake status, classification backlog, program waitlists, overtime hours.

### A.3 Municipal permitting and development review
- Usual constraint: senior plan reviewers or engineers in one discipline; sometimes inter-departmental sign-off.
- Policy constraints: accepting incomplete submittals; sequential review across departments; late-hit comments; review standards in conflict.
- Exploit: Front Door completeness check; pre-application meetings or MOUs for study parameters (Columbus Traffic MOU [Summary]); coordinator roles; concurrent review; cut-off for late comments (Columbus 3P Stage 2A [Summary]).
- Subordinate: release to review only when Full-Kit; resubmittal triage; publish a customer-facing checklist.
- Measures: first-time-go rate, review cycles per permit, days with the agency vs days with the applicant, percent within ordinance clock.

### A.4 Environmental regulators (DEQ-type agencies)
- Usual constraints: technical specialists (hydrology, modeling, toxicology); public notice timing; financial assurance review.
- Policy constraints: linear review sequences; information scarcity from consultants; paper records.
- Exploit: constraint-within-the-constraint focus (WY DEQ Coal hydrology and CHIA [Summary]); completeness review milestones separate from technical review (WY DEQ LQD-URP: 60-day completeness and 150-day technical review milestones [Summary]); automated Front Door checks.
- Measures: completeness determination time, technical review time, deficiency rounds, backlog by permit type.

### A.5 Utilities and special districts
- Usual constraint: development review engineers; inspection crews; system modeling staff.
- Exploit: qualified screening before engineers (Santa Clara Valley Water [Summary]); dedicated engineer hours; requirements captured in the permitting platform.
- Measures: engineer hours on technical work, requests returned for incompleteness, cycle time by request type.

### A.6 Benefits, eligibility, and licensing
- Usual constraints: eligibility determiners, adjudicators, counselors, licensing specialists.
- Policy constraints: denial as the only tool for incomplete work (which triggers appeals); the clock starting before Full-Kit; centralized approvals for routine decisions.
- Exploit: pending-completion status; application navigation separated from eligibility determination (OWBC future state [Summary]); aged-case review (NH VR [Summary]); automation of administrative tasks so counselors focus on guidance.
- Measures: first-time-go, days to determination, appeals rate, aged cases.

### A.7 Legal offices
- Usual constraint: the chief legal officer's or senior attorneys' review and signature.
- Policy constraints: everything routed to the top; constant status-check interruptions.
- Exploit: categorize work by type (Greeley three buckets [Summary]); confirmation briefs and back-briefs; paralegal routing with standard criteria; style guides to reduce review rounds.
- Measures: volume by type, turnaround by type, review rounds per document.

### A.8 Public-sector IT
- Usual constraint: development teams or a specific skill (database, security review).
- Policy constraints: starting every approved project at once; priority by escalation.
- Exploit and subordinate: choke and release into development (SD BIT [Summary]); a single prioritized portfolio; CCPM pipelining.
- Measures: projects in progress per team, throughput of completed projects, cycle time from approval to release.

## Appendix B. Worked QT/OE scenario comparison (hypothetical example)

A regulatory unit completes 80 permits per month; 90% meet quality criteria (no reopen, legally sufficient). Monthly operating expense for the value stream is $64,000.

- Baseline QT = 0.90 x 80 = 72 quality units. QT/OE = 72 / 64,000 = 0.001125 units per dollar, or about $889 per quality unit.
- Scenario 1 (exploit): Full-Kit gate and coordinator role. Throughput rises to 92 permits; quality to 95%; OE rises to $66,000 (coordinator partly reassigned). QT = 87.4. Cost per quality unit = 66,000 / 87.4 = about $755. QT/OE improves by (87.4 / 66,000) / (72 / 64,000) - 1 = about 17.7%.
- Scenario 2 (elevate): add one reviewer without process change. Throughput rises to 96; quality stays 90%; OE rises to $73,000. QT = 86.4. Cost per quality unit = about $845. QT/OE improves by about 5.2%.
- Interpretation: in this illustration, exploiting the constraint yields more value per dollar than elevating first. A real comparison requires sourced data, and both options may ultimately be needed.

All figures in this appendix are invented for teaching and must never appear in client deliverables as data.
