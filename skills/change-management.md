# Change Management for Public-Sector Engagements (ReEngine Consulting)

## 0. When to use this skill

Use when:
- A redesigned process, new SOP set, new system, reorganization, or policy change must be adopted by people who did not ask for it.
- Staff or managers resist, comply minimally, or revert.
- A sponsor is passive, absent, or sending mixed signals.
- Results from a kaizen or recommendations report are not sticking.
- A solicitation requires organizational change management, communication planning, training, or stakeholder engagement.
- You need an ADKAR diagnosis for a group or an individual.
- Changes touch unions, civil service rules, sworn personnel, or elected officials.

Default stance for ReEngine: every GQI engagement is a change effort. Launch It is the formal change event, but change management starts at Charter It (sponsorship, awareness) and continues through Track It (reinforcement). Technical solutions and people-side solutions are planned together.

## 2. Core concepts

### 2.1 Why change management

Organizations change one person at a time. A new process produces results only when the people who do the work adopt it (speed of adoption, ultimate utilization, proficiency). Change management is the structured application of process and tools to lead the people side of change to achieve a required outcome.

Prosci frames the result as: project benefits depend on adoption and usage. Its longitudinal Best Practices research reports that projects with excellent change management are several times more likely to meet or exceed objectives than those with poor change management (commonly cited as about six to seven times); verify the current figure before quoting externally.

### 2.2 Prosci PCT Model

Four elements of a successful change:
- Success: a clear definition of what success looks like (organizational benefits and individual adoption).
- Leadership / Sponsorship: authorizes, funds, and visibly champions the change.
- Project Management: the technical side (design, develop, deliver).
- Change Management: the people side (prepare, equip, support).
Assess each on a scale; low scores flag risk. In government, sponsorship is often fragmented (elected official, agency head, division director) and must be aligned.

### 2.3 Prosci 3-Phase Process (Prosci Methodology)

| Phase | Purpose | Key activities | Outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare Approach | Define success, impact, and approach | Define success (objectives, benefits, adoption metrics); define impact (groups affected and how, change impact assessment); define approach (change characteristics and organizational attributes risk assessment; sponsor and coalition model; team structure) | Change strategy; impact assessment; risk assessment; sponsor model |
| 2. Manage Change | Plan and act; track; adapt | Plans using the five levers (communications, sponsorship, coaching, training, resistance management); track ADKAR progress, adoption and usage; adapt actions | Integrated change plans; ADKAR assessments; adoption metrics |
| 3. Sustain Outcomes | Review, activate sustainment, transfer ownership | Review performance against success; activate reinforcement; transfer ownership to operations | Sustainment plan; handoff |

### 2.4 ADKAR

Individual change model; sequential elements:
- Awareness of the need for change (why, why now, what is the risk of not changing).
- Desire to participate and support the change (personal choice influenced by motivators).
- Knowledge of how to change (what to do during and after the transition).
- Ability to implement required skills and behaviors (demonstrated performance).
- Reinforcement to sustain the change (recognition, rewards, accountability, measurement).

Barrier point: the first element in sequence that scores 3 or lower on a 1-5 scale. Address the barrier point before moving on; training (Knowledge) will not help someone who lacks Desire.

ADKAR element detail with public-sector tactics:

| Element | Drivers | Signs it is missing | Tactics (public sector) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Understanding the nature of the change and the reason; perceived problems; credibility of the messenger; misinformation; contestability | "Why are we doing this?"; rumors; "this is another fad"; "we've always done it this way" (WWADI) | Sponsor explains business reasons in mission terms (applicants waiting, audit findings, statutory clocks, public trust); share data (baselines, backlogs, customer feedback); supervisors repeat in team meetings; gemba walks to see the problem; make the risk of not changing explicit |
| Desire | Nature of the change (how it affects me); organizational context; individual situation; personal motivators | Passive non-participation; "what's in it for me?"; fear of job loss; fear of accountability | Involve staff in design (kaizen); address job security honestly (sponsor redeployment commitment if true); show how the change removes frustrations (Red Steps, rework, interruptions); engage union stewards early; identify champions among respected peers; listen to and address concerns; leverage professional values (protecting deep-work time for attorneys) |
| Knowledge | Current knowledge base; capacity to learn; resources for training; access to information | "I don't know how"; workarounds | Role-based training; SOPs and knowledge cards; job aids; sandbox simulations; train-the-trainer; just-in-time training |
| Ability | Psychological blocks; physical abilities; intellectual capability; time to develop skills; resources | Knows but cannot do it consistently; errors in early use | Practice and coaching; pilots; floor support; reduced workload during transition; feedback; time to build skill; peer support |
| Reinforcement | Degree to which reinforcement is meaningful; association with progress; absence of negative consequences; accountability systems | Reverting to old ways; "it didn't stick" | Recognition by sponsor and supervisors; dashboards and huddles; leader standard work; incorporate into performance expectations (within civil service and CBA rules); celebrate early wins; remove old systems and forms; audit adherence |

ADKAR diagnostic questions:
- Awareness: "Why do you think this change is happening? What happens if we don't change?"
- Desire: "How do you feel about it? What would make it worthwhile for you? What concerns you?"
- Knowledge: "Do you know what you'll do differently? What information or training do you need?"
- Ability: "Have you been able to do it on real work? What gets in the way?"
- Reinforcement: "What would make it stick? What happens when someone goes back to the old way?"

### 2.5 Sponsorship: the ABCs

Effective sponsors are:
- Active and visible participation throughout the project (not just kickoff).
- Build a coalition of sponsorship with peers and managers (the sponsor coalition).
- Communicate directly with employees about the reasons for the change.
Sponsor roadmap: activities by phase (kickoff message, attend report-outs, steering decisions, recognize wins, hold managers accountable). Sponsor mistakes: delegating sponsorship to the consultant; disappearing after kickoff; failing to align peers; sending mixed signals by tolerating the old way.

Public-sector sponsor coalition: elected official or board (authorization), agency head (organizational authority), division directors (operational authority), union leadership (labor relations partner), HR and legal (enablers). Moore's Strategic Triangle (BOK paper): public value, legitimacy and support, and operational capacity must align; change plans must address the authorizing environment as well as staff.

Horseshoe Bay lesson: ReEngine identified "change from the side": expert advice is often the hardest to take (the "3-cornered dichotomy"), and success required department leaders (for example the fire chief) to champion the change from the top so it did not feel alien to staff [Summary].

### 2.6 People managers as coaches

Five roles of people managers (Prosci): communicator, advocate, coach, liaison, resistance manager. Managers are the preferred senders of messages about personal impact ("what does this mean for me?"), while senior leaders are preferred for business reasons. Prepare managers first: they must move through their own ADKAR before coaching staff. Provide manager toolkits (talking points, FAQ, ADKAR conversation guide, escalation path).

### 2.7 The five levers (Prosci)

| Lever | Purpose | Public-sector notes |
|---|---|---|
| Communications | Build awareness and share information | Two-way; sender matters (sponsor for why; supervisor for personal impact); repeat 5-7 times through multiple channels; mind public records and open meetings |
| Sponsorship | Visible authority and commitment | Align elected and appointed leaders; brief new leaders after transitions |
| Coaching | Managers support individuals | Equip supervisors; one-on-ones; huddles |
| Training | Knowledge and ability | Role-based; aligned to SOPs; train-the-trainer for scale (ND IT T3 model [Summary]) |
| Resistance management | Prevent, identify, address resistance | Root cause of resistance via ADKAR; involve unions; address fears honestly |

### 2.8 Change impact assessment

For each affected group, assess degree of impact (low, medium, high) on ten aspects (Prosci list): processes, systems, tools, job roles, critical behaviors, mindsets, attitudes and beliefs, reporting structure, performance reviews, compensation, location. In government add: legal authority and delegations, public-facing commitments, and union-covered working conditions.

Impact assessment template:

| Group | Size | Processes | Systems | Tools | Job roles | Critical behaviors | Mindset | Reporting | Performance reviews | Compensation | Location | Overall | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 2.9 Risk assessment: change characteristics and organizational attributes

Change characteristics (score each low to high): scope (workgroup to enterprise), number impacted, variation in impact across groups, degree of process, system, job role, and reporting changes, timeframe, amount of change already underway.
Organizational attributes: perceived need for change, impact of past changes, change capacity (saturation), past change management competency, shared vision, resource and financial health, culture and responsiveness, organizational reinforcement, leadership style and power distribution, executive and middle management change competency, sponsorship.
Plot on a 2x2: large change in a change-resistant organization is high risk and needs a more intensive approach.

Public-sector attributes to watch: change saturation (many initiatives at once); history of failed initiatives ("flavor of the month"); risk aversion after audits (NH VR: staff hesitant to close cases or decide because of previous audit findings, necessitating a culture of risk acceptance built through standard work [Summary]); distrust from unreliable systems (SD AG: distrust and resistance where duplication of effort had been a coping mechanism for system unreliability [Summary]).

### 2.10 Readiness assessment

Survey or interview-based readiness for each group: awareness of the change, perceived need, leadership support, capacity, prior experience, concerns. Repeat at milestones. Combine with ADKAR pulse checks.

## 3. Resistance management

### 3.1 Principles

- Resistance is a normal reaction to change, not a character flaw. Expect it, plan for it, and listen to it; it often contains design information.
- Most resistance can be prevented with good sponsorship, early awareness, and involvement.
- Find the root cause using ADKAR (missing Awareness, missing Desire, Knowledge or Ability gaps, or no Reinforcement).
- Managers are the front line of resistance management.

### 3.2 Common sources in government

| Source | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of awareness of why | "Nobody told us the backlog was a problem" | Share data, customer stories, audit findings; sponsor message |
| Fear of job loss or reduced hours | "Lean means layoffs" | Sponsor commitment on redeployment if true; show capacity goes to backlog or service |
| Loss of control or status | Expert gatekeepers become a team-based model (SD AG support staff single points of failure [Summary]) | Involve in design; new valued roles (trainers, SMEs) |
| Professional identity | Attorneys resist "efficiency" metrics (Greeley [Summary]); engineers resist checklists | Frame as protecting deep work and professional judgment |
| Past failures | "We mapped this five years ago and nothing happened" | Show follow-through: 30/60/90 report-outs; quick wins; BRAG visibility |
| Distrust of systems | Duplicate tracking as a coping mechanism (SD AG [Summary]) | Fix reliability first; pilot; keep a temporary safety net with a sunset |
| Risk aversion after audits | Fear of making decisions (NH VR [Summary]) | Standard work with clear decision criteria and authority; leadership backing |
| Workload saturation | "We don't have time to improve" (Columbus Traffic at 200% workload [Summary]) | Short sessions; hybrid model; remove work first; quick wins that save time |
| Cultural shift in duties | Pittsburgh: shifting evidence transport duties was a significant cultural change for frontline officers [Summary] | Involve command staff and officers; clear SOPs; explain chain-of-custody rationale |
| Group conflict around the initiative | Ratner (BOK): Lean in a public department divided staff into groups and later became a rallying point against a restructuring | Broad participation; avoid "in-group" of Lean enthusiasts; separate CI from restructuring |
| Accountability anxiety | Fear that higher standards increase turnover (OWBC HR hiring [Summary]) | Show how a quality process improves retention; supportive accountability. Confirm with David before external use |

### 3.3 Resistance management steps

1. Anticipate: list likely resistance by group from the impact assessment.
2. Prevent: early sponsor communication; involvement; manager preparation.
3. Identify: listen in huddles, pulse surveys, one-on-ones, adoption data.
4. Diagnose with ADKAR.
5. Respond: tactic matched to barrier point (Section 2.4).
6. Escalate persistent resistance to the sponsor with facts, not names in group settings.
7. Reinforce adopters visibly.

### 3.4 Resistance conversation script (supervisor)

"I've noticed you're still using the old intake spreadsheet. Help me understand what's going on. [Listen.] What's making the new process harder? [Listen for A, D, K, A, or R.] Here's why it matters for the people we serve: [business reason]. What would help you make the switch: more training, a walkthrough on real files, or fixing something in the process? Let's agree what you'll try this week, and I'll check in Friday."

## 4. Other change models

### 4.1 Kotter's 8 steps (Leading Change; later "accelerators")

1. Create a sense of urgency.
2. Build a guiding coalition.
3. Form a strategic vision and initiatives.
4. Enlist a volunteer army (communicate the vision).
5. Enable action by removing barriers.
6. Generate short-term wins.
7. Sustain acceleration (consolidate gains).
8. Institute change (anchor in culture).
Public-sector notes: urgency can come from audits, lawsuits, legislative mandates, backlog stories, or public safety events; avoid manufactured crisis. Guiding coalitions should include labor and middle managers. Short-term wins matter more in government because skepticism is high and political cycles are short.

Wirth's traditional change methodology (BOK) parallels Kotter's eight stages: establishing the need, forming a coalition, creating and communicating a vision, empowering action, generating wins, consolidating, and institutionalizing; use it as an internal checklist when a client already knows Kotter.

### 4.2 Lewin: unfreeze, change, refreeze

- Unfreeze: create readiness (disconfirmation of the status quo, psychological safety).
- Change (move): implement new behaviors, structures, processes.
- Refreeze: stabilize with norms, policies, and reinforcement.
Force-field analysis: list driving and restraining forces with strengths; strengthen drivers and, more effectively, reduce restrainers.

Force-field template:

| Driving forces | Strength (1-5) | Restraining forces | Strength (1-5) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### 4.3 Bridges: managing transitions

Change is situational (the new process); transition is psychological. Three phases:
1. Ending, losing, letting go: acknowledge losses (expertise, status, relationships, familiar routines). Mark the ending (retire the old form ceremonially).
2. Neutral zone: confusion and creativity; temporary systems; more communication; opportunities for innovation.
3. New beginning: purpose, picture, plan, part (the 4 Ps).
Public-sector notes: long-tenured staff may lose identity attached to legacy processes (CalGEM district-specific habits [Summary]); honor the past ("this process served us well when...") before moving on.

### 4.4 McKinsey 7S

Hard elements: strategy, structure, systems. Soft elements: shared values (center), style, staff, skills. A change in one requires alignment of the others.

Diagnostic template:

| Element | Current state | Desired state | Gap | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Structure | | | | |

Use 7S for reorganizations (for example NYSDOT ODO structure and the decision brief on a new compliance role [Summary]) and for program startups (Clean Energy Fund of Texas building an audit-ready grant entity [Summary]; confirm with David before external use).

### 4.5 Choosing a model

| Need | Model |
|---|---|
| Individual adoption diagnosis | ADKAR |
| Project-level structured OCM plan | Prosci 3-phase |
| Large organizational transformation; leadership narrative | Kotter |
| Simple framing for a team | Lewin |
| Grief, loss, identity issues | Bridges |
| Alignment across structure, systems, culture | McKinsey 7S |
| Dilemmas and hidden conflicts | TOC evaporating cloud (theory-of-constraints.md) |

## 5. Public-sector context

### 5.1 Unions and labor relations

- Know the collective bargaining agreement (CBA): changes to duties, schedules, workload standards, technology, or evaluation criteria may require notice, meet-and-confer, or bargaining.
- Engage union leadership early as partners: share the goal (service, workload relief), invite stewards to kaizen events, agree on principles (no layoffs due to improvement if the employer commits to it).
- Avoid framing in productivity-per-person terms; frame around removing frustration, rework, and backlog.
- Document agreements through HR and labor relations, not the consultant.

### 5.2 Civil service and HR rules

- Job classifications limit reassigning duties; new roles (coordinators, SMEs) may require reclassification.
- Performance evaluation changes follow HR policy; reinforcement through performance reviews must respect these rules.
- Hiring timelines are long; plan staffing changes accordingly.
- Training time may need approval and backfill.

### 5.3 Elected and appointed officials

- Elected officials authorize and fund; they respond to constituents and media.
- Brief them with outcomes and public value, not methodology.
- Time decisions to legislative or budget calendars; process mapping can provide evidence for funding decisions even when the designed solution is deferred (Minnesota childcare [Summary]).
- Political turnover: build durable coalitions across career leadership; document decisions.

### 5.4 Professional and sworn cultures

- Attorneys, engineers, scientists, physicians: frame around professional judgment and protecting expert time. Greeley: attorneys resist efficiency metrics; tools were framed as protecting deep work from administrative interruptions [Summary].
- Police and corrections: command structures help with authorization but adoption depends on line supervisors; respect operational safety and legal requirements (chain of custody as the governing requirement in Pittsburgh [Summary]).
- Fire and EMS: readiness and training culture; data on training hours vs call volume (Horseshoe Bay Fire Strategic Path [Summary]).

### 5.5 Public transparency

Communications may be public records; assume internal messages can be read by the public and media. Coordinate with public information officers for external-facing changes (applicants, permit customers). Prepare customer communications for process changes (new checklists, portals, status definitions).

### 5.6 Creativity first

ND Parks and Recreation lesson: jumping straight into process mapping can feel like another administrative burden; starting with "Thinking Unconstrained" creativity training earned staff buy-in by showing how the tools save time and frustration [Summary]. Consider opening an engagement with a short creativity or problem-solving session before mapping, especially in field-based workforces.

## 8. Metrics

Three levels (Prosci framing):
1. Organizational performance: the project's outcomes (cycle time, first-time-go, backlog, QT/OE). Use the Track It dashboard.
2. Individual performance (adoption): speed of adoption (time to reach target usage), ultimate utilization (percent of intended users using the new way), proficiency (error rates, audit adherence).
3. Change management performance: activities completed (sponsor roadmap, communications, training), ADKAR scores by group over time, resistance issues logged and resolved, manager toolkit usage.

Example adoption metrics (hypothetical example):
- Percent of applications processed through the new Full-Kit checklist (target 95% by week 6).
- Percent of staff passing proficiency check on new SOP (target 90% before go-live).
- Old spreadsheet usage (target zero after sunset date).
- ADKAR pulse average by element (track barrier point shifts).
- BRAG status of change actions (do not rush to Blue).

Leading indicators: training attendance and proficiency, manager check-ins completed, pulse-survey Awareness and Desire scores, questions logged. Lagging indicators: adoption rates, outcome metrics, reversion incidents.

## 11. Decision guides

### 11.1 How much change management?

| Change size | Organizational readiness | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Small (one team, minor process) | High | Light: supervisor communication, job aid, huddle reinforcement |
| Small | Low | Moderate: add sponsor message, involvement, check-ins |
| Large (multiple units, roles, systems) | High | Moderate to full Prosci plan |
| Large | Low (saturation, distrust) | Full plan: sponsor coalition, impact assessment, manager coaching, training, resistance plan, sustainment; consider phasing |

### 11.2 What is the barrier point?

Run the ADKAR assessment (Section 2.4). Typical public-sector patterns: Awareness weak at the front line when leaders have data staff never see; Desire weak where job security, workload, or professional identity is threatened; Knowledge and Ability weak with system go-lives; Reinforcement weak after consultants leave.

### 11.3 Who should send this message?

Business reasons and direction: senior sponsor. Personal impact and day-to-day: immediate supervisor. Technical details: SME or trainer. External (applicants, public): agency public information function.

### 11.4 Is it resistance or a design problem?

If many people struggle at the same step, it is probably a design problem; fix the process. If resistance follows a pattern by group or role, diagnose ADKAR for that group. If one individual resists, coach one-on-one.

## 12. Facilitation scripts and questions

Sponsor kickoff message (draft structure):
"Here's what's happening: [change]. Here's why: [mission reason, data]. Here's what happens if we don't: [risk]. Here's what it means for you: [high level; your supervisor will go deeper]. Here's what won't change: [values, job security if committed]. Here's how you'll be involved: [kaizen, feedback]. Here's how to raise concerns: [channel]. I'm committed to this, and I'll be back at [milestone] to hear how it's going."

Opening a session with skeptical staff:
"You may have seen improvement efforts come and go. Fair enough. Here's what's different this time: you're designing it, leadership has committed to [decisions on the spot / 30-60-90 follow-up], and we'll make progress visible on a board everyone can see."

Acknowledging loss (Bridges):
"The current process was built by people in this room, and it has served the public for years. We're not saying it was wrong; the demands changed. What would you want to keep from how we do it today?"

Union partnership:
"Our shared interest is a workload people can sustain and service the public deserves. We'd like your stewards in the room when we design, and we'll route anything touching working conditions through labor relations before it moves."

Elected-official briefing:
"In one sentence: [outcome for constituents]. Here's the evidence: [one chart]. Here's what we need from you: [decision, funding, policy]. Here's when you'll see results: [date], and here's how we'll report."

Pulse check questions:
- On a scale of 1-5, how well do you understand why this change is happening?
- How much do you want this change to succeed?
- How confident are you that you know what to do differently?
- How confident are you that you can do it on real work?
- How likely is it that the change will stick here?
- What would help most right now?

## 13. Common pitfalls

1. Treating communication as change management (announcements without involvement, coaching, or reinforcement).
2. Training before Desire exists.
3. Sponsor absent after kickoff; delegating sponsorship to consultants.
4. Skipping middle managers; they then block quietly.
5. Promising "no layoffs" without authority to commit.
6. Ignoring unions until a grievance.
7. Creating an in-group of Lean champions and an out-group (Ratner).
8. Launching during peak workload or change saturation.
9. Declaring success at go-live; no reinforcement plan.
10. Leaving the old way available indefinitely.
11. Measuring activity (emails sent) instead of adoption.
12. Ignoring the authorizing environment (board, legislature, public).
13. Naming individuals as resistors in shared documents.
14. Using generic change jargon with professionals; frame in their values.

## 16. Glossary

- ADKAR: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement.
- Adoption metrics: speed of adoption, ultimate utilization, proficiency.
- Authorizing environment: elected officials, boards, oversight bodies, and the public who grant legitimacy and resources.
- Barrier point: first ADKAR element scoring 3 or lower.
- Change agent / champion: respected peer supporting change locally.
- Change impact assessment: analysis of how each group is affected.
- Change saturation: too many concurrent changes for capacity.
- Five levers: communications, sponsorship, coaching, training, resistance management.
- Force-field analysis: driving vs restraining forces (Lewin).
- Neutral zone: Bridges' in-between phase.
- PCT model: Prosci model of success, leadership/sponsorship, project management, change management.
- Prosci 3-phase process: Prepare Approach, Manage Change, Sustain Outcomes.
- Reinforcement: mechanisms that sustain change.
- Sponsor ABCs: Active and visible participation, Build a coalition, Communicate directly.
- Sponsor coalition: aligned leaders across the organization.
- 7S: strategy, structure, systems, shared values, style, staff, skills.
- WWADI: We've Always Done It.

## Appendix A. Worked OCM plan outline: permitting process and portal go-live (hypothetical example)

Context (hypothetical): a city replaces email-based permit intake with a portal plus a Full-Kit checklist; reviewers shift to concurrent review; a new coordinator role triages submittals.

1. Success definition: 90% of submittals through the portal within 8 weeks; first-time-go up from baseline (to be measured); reviewers using the shared queue daily; customer complaints about "lost" submittals down.
2. Impacted groups: intake clerks (high: process, system, job role), reviewers (medium: process, system), coordinator (new role: high), applicants and design professionals (high: process, tools), IT (medium), supervisors (medium: behaviors, reporting).
3. Risk: large change, moderate readiness (prior system go-live remembered as painful).
4. Sponsor coalition: city manager (authorization), development services director (primary sponsor), IT director, public works and utilities directors (concurrent reviewers), HR (coordinator classification), union representative for clerks.
5. Levers:
   - Communications: sponsor message at week 0; supervisor team meetings; applicant notices and webinars 6 and 2 weeks before go-live; FAQ.
   - Sponsorship: director attends training openings and the first two weekly huddles.
   - Coaching: supervisor toolkit; daily floor support first 2 weeks.
   - Training: role-based sessions with sandbox practice on real-looking files; job aids; proficiency check.
   - Resistance: anticipated clerk concerns (job role change), reviewer concerns (queue visibility as surveillance); responses via involvement in design and clear purpose statements.
6. Reinforcement: dashboard of portal share and first-time-go; recognition at monthly all-hands; email intake retired on a published date with an exception process.
7. Metrics: ADKAR pulse at weeks -4, 0, +4, +8; adoption and outcome metrics weekly.
All numbers and roles above are illustrative and must not be presented as a real engagement.

## Appendix D. Change patterns by public-sector domain

- Courts: judges are independent officers; changes to calendars and procedures need judicial buy-in, often through the presiding judge and court administrator. Involve clerks, prosecutors, defense, and law enforcement as partners. Simulations such as a WGLL time-allocation game help a judge see options without feeling directed (Albany used one [Summary]).
- Corrections: shift-based workforces need communication across all shifts; command staff authorize but line supervisors drive adoption; safety rationale is a strong driver.
- Police: chain of command, union contracts, and legal requirements (evidence, use of force reporting) shape change; involve sergeants as coaches.
- Municipal permitting: applicants and design professionals are external adopters; communicate early, run webinars, publish checklists; reviewers may fear transparency of queues as surveillance; frame visibility as protection against unfair blame.
- Environmental regulators: scientists and engineers value technical rigor; show the logic and data; respect statutory review standards; cross-program silos may require a unified map to show interdependence (WY DEQ LQD-URP [Summary]).
- Utilities and special districts: boards and ratepayers form the authorizing environment; operations culture values reliability; pilots in one division reduce risk.
- Benefits and eligibility: front-line staff often fear audit findings; standard work with clear authority reduces risk aversion (NH VR [Summary]); customers may need navigators.
- Legal offices: frame around protecting deep work, consistent quality, and reducing interruptions (Greeley [Summary]).
- Higher education and state systems: shared governance and campus autonomy require coalitions across institutions (SD Board of Regents system Journey Board [Summary]).
- Statewide enterprise CI programs: sustainability depends on internal trainers and leadership routines (ND IT train-the-trainer [Summary]); avoid dependence on consultants.

## Appendix E. Change readiness pulse survey (template)

Rate 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree):
1. I understand why this change is happening. (Awareness)
2. Leadership has explained what happens if we do not change. (Awareness)
3. I support this change. (Desire)
4. My concerns have been heard. (Desire)
5. I know what I will need to do differently. (Knowledge)
6. I have had enough training or information. (Knowledge)
7. I have been able to do the new process on real work. (Ability)
8. I have the time and tools to work the new way. (Ability)
9. Leaders and supervisors notice and recognize the new way of working. (Reinforcement)
10. I believe this change will last. (Reinforcement)
Open questions: What is going well? What is getting in the way? What would help most?
Report results by group (minimum group size to protect anonymity, for example 5); never by individual.
